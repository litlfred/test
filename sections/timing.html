Optimal timing for routine MCV1 and MCV2

Measles vaccines: WHO position paper – April 2017, journal pp. 221–222 · 21 recommendation(s), 5 remark(s). Every statement and remark is verbatim from the English column of the PDF; the build fails if it is not found on the page it cites.

R07

In countries with ongoing transmission in which the risk of measles mortality among infants remains high, MCV1 should be administered at 9 months of age.

Condition: In countries with ongoing transmission in which the risk of measles mortality among infants remains high

journal p.221 · PDF page 17 · WER9217-R07

Remark R07-1 · journal p.221 · PDF page 17

In these settings, on-time delivery of MCV1 is important to ensure optimal protection during the susceptible period in infancy.

R08

These countries should administer the routine dose of MCV2 at age 15–18 months.

Condition: In countries with ongoing transmission in which the risk of measles mortality among infants remains high

journal p.221 · PDF page 17 · refines R07 · WER9217-R08

R09

The minimum interval between MCV1 and MCV2 is 4 weeks.

journal p.221 · PDF page 17 · WER9217-R09

R10

In countries with low levels of measles transmission (i.e. those nearing measles elimination or verified as having eliminated endemic measles virus transmission) and therefore the risk of measles virus infection among infants is low, MCV1 may be administered at 12 months of age to take advantage of the higher seroconversion rates achieved at this age.

Condition: In countries with low levels of measles transmission

journal p.221 · PDF page 17 · WER9217-R10

Remark R10-1 · journal p.221 · PDF page 17

However, before implementing this change, policy-makers should review local data on the age at which infants actually receive measles vaccine, the coverage expected at 12 months compared with 9 months, and the age-specific measles incidence.

R11

In countries where MCV1 is administered at 12 months of age, the optimal age for delivering routine MCV2 is based on programmatic considerations that achieve the highest coverage of MCV2 and, hence, the highest population immunity.

Condition: In countries where MCV1 is administered at 12 months of age

journal p.221 · PDF page 17 · refines R10 · WER9217-R11

Remark R11-1 · journal p.221 · PDF page 17

Administration of MCV2 at 15–18 months of age ensures early protection of the individual, slows the accumulation of susceptible young children, may correspond to the schedule for other routine immunizations (for example, a DTP booster, PCV, or meningococcal vaccines).

R12

If MCV1 coverage is high (>90%) and school enrolment is high (>95%), administration of routine MCV2 at school entry may prove an effective strategy for achieving high coverage and preventing outbreaks in schools.

Condition: If MCV1 coverage is high (>90%) and school enrolment is high (>95%)

R13

In the following situations, a supplementary dose of MCV should be given to infants from 6 months of age:

during a measles outbreak as part of intensified service delivery;

R13.2

during campaigns in settings where the risk of measles among infants < 9 months of age remains high (e.g. in endemic countries experiencing regular outbreaks);

for internally displaced populations and refugees, and populations in conflict zones;

for individual infants at high risk of contracting measles (e.g. contacts of known measles cases or in settings with increased risk of exposure during outbreaks such as day-care facilities);

R14

MCV administered before 9 months of age should therefore be considered a supplementary dose and recorded on the child’s vaccination record as “MCV0” unless the country has data showing high seroconversion when vaccination is carried out before 9 months of age.

journal p.222 · PDF page 18 · WER9217-R14

Remark R14-1 · journal p.222 · PDF page 18

Measles vaccine immunogenicity and effectiveness are lower at 6 months than at later ages, and there are concerns about the long-term effectiveness of an early 2-dose schedule and its potential for later blunting of immunity.

R15

Children who receive a MCV0 dose should also receive MCV1 and MCV2 at the recommended ages according to the national schedule.

journal p.222 · PDF page 18 · refines R13 · WER9217-R15

R16

Countries using MR or MMR in their national schedule should use the combined vaccine rather than measles-only formulations in all children, including those aged <1 year.

Condition: Countries using MR or MMR in their national schedule

journal p.222 · PDF page 18 · WER9217-R16

Remark R16-1 · journal p.222 · PDF page 18

Available evidence on safety and immunogenicity of rubella and mumps-containing vaccines support their use from 6 months of age.

R17

Because many cases of measles occur in children aged >12 months who have not been vaccinated, routine delivery of MCV1 should not be limited to infants aged 9–12 months and routine delivery of MCV2 should not be limited to infants aged 15–18 months of age.

journal p.222 · PDF page 18 · WER9217-R17

R18

Every opportunity (e.g. whenever children come in contact with health services) should be taken to vaccinate all children who missed one or both MCV routine doses, particularly those <15 years of age.

journal p.222 · PDF page 18 · WER9217-R18

R19

Policies prohibiting the use of the vaccine in children >1 year of age, older children and adolescents should be changed to allow them to be vaccinated as necessary.

journal p.222 · PDF page 18 · WER9217-R19

R20

Irrespective of the strategy or schedule, the child’s immunization card and the clinic’s vaccination register should be designed to allow accurate recording of supplementary (MCV0), routine (MCV1 and MCV2) and campaign doses.

journal p.222 · PDF page 18 · WER9217-R20

R21

Children should be screened for their measles vaccination history at the time of school entry, and those lacking evidence of receipt of 2 doses should be vaccinated with any missing doses.
